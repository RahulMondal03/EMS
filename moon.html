Earth · Satellite I

The Moon

One rock, 384,400 kilometres away, locked so that we only ever see one side of it. Here is what it measures, how it changes shape, and what the dark patches are.

What it is

The Moon is Earth's only natural satellite and the fifth largest in the solar system — a quarter of Earth's diameter, which makes it unusually big relative to the planet it orbits. It most likely formed about 4.5 billion years ago, when a Mars-sized body struck the young Earth and the debris settled into orbit.

It has no meaningful atmosphere, so nothing moderates its temperature and nothing erodes its surface. Footprints left in 1969 are still there, sharp-edged, and the daytime and night-time temperatures differ by roughly 300 °C.

The cycle of phases

Phases are not shadows cast by Earth. Half the Moon is always sunlit; what changes is how much of that lit half faces us as the Moon works around its orbit. One full round trip takes 29.5 days.

The dark patches

The grey areas visible without a telescope are maria — "seas" — named by seventeenth-century astronomers who took them for water. They are basalt plains, ancient impact basins flooded by lava and then frozen, which is why they are darker and smoother than the cratered highlands around them.

  • Mare Tranquillitatis Sea of Tranquillity — where Apollo 11 landed on 20 July 1969.
  • Oceanus Procellarum Ocean of Storms — the largest of them, spanning some 2,500 km.
  • Mare Imbrium Sea of Showers — a basin gouged out by an impact roughly 3.9 billion years ago.
  • Mare Serenitatis Sea of Serenity — Apollo 17 worked its south-eastern edge in 1972.
  • Mare Crisium Sea of Crises — an isolated oval near the eastern limb, easy to pick out.

The Moon is drifting away from us at about 3.8 cm a year, measured by bouncing lasers off reflectors the Apollo crews left behind. Tidal friction is slowly handing Earth's rotational energy to the Moon's orbit, and in the same exchange our days are getting longer.
